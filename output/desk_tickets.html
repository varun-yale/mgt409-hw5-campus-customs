1. How the agents did on each ticket

Ticket 101: right answer, but Accounting was shallow

Inventory did the important work. It found size S at 0, followed that to invoice 501, saw the invoice was open and 3 days overdue, looked up Bulldog Print Co's 5-day lead time, and then refused to count the reprint as incoming stock because nobody had paid for it. Customer Service wrote the kind of reply I'd actually send: no S right now, no restock date, but M (5), L (3) and XL (2) are on the shelf. The Boss turned that into one clean decision and prepared exactly one payment, $840.00 to Bulldog Print Co.

The weak spot was Accounting. The Boss routed it in directly, and it only called get_shop_date and get_invoice_status. It never checked the cash balance or what else was owed, so nobody on the team mentioned that $2,400 rent was due from the same account two days later. It worked out ($3,400 − $840 = $2,560), but the "can we afford this" check really came from me clicking approve and the backend's no-negative rule, not from the agent whose whole job is money. Inventory also called get_invoice_status twice. Because specialists don't see each other's reports, routing Accounting directly also removed the reason for anyone to delegate.

Ticket 102: the best teamwork, plus some redundancy

This is the run where delegation actually meant something. Facilities pulled the lease ($2,400, due 2026-09-02, two days from the shop date) and asked Accounting what else was owed out of checking before calling the balance safe, which is exactly what its prompt says to do. Accounting answered with $2,560 and no open invoices (invoice 501 was already paid on 101), so both agents flagged that only $160 would be left. The Boss prepared the $2,400 rent and I approved it.

It wasn't efficient, though. The Boss also routed Accounting directly, so Accounting wrote its own report and asked Facilities the same lease question Facilities had already answered. While answering Facilities, Accounting even tried to ask Facilities back, and ask_colleague refused with "facilities is already in this chain." That's the loop guard doing its job in a real run. It also shows that a fully connected team naturally makes circular asks, so the guard was necessary rather than a nice extra.

Ticket 103: messy, but it failed safely

The base facts were right: Inventory found 8 of the 20 medium hoodies (12 short), and Accounting found the $22 cost, $58 list price and 62.07% break-even. Then it got complicated. Customer Service asked Accounting for the price floor, which was fine, and then asked the Boss for a decision. While answering, the Boss asked Inventory and Accounting all over again. Inventory tried to ask Customer Service, refused as a loop. Accounting tried to ask Facilities about rent, refused at the depth limit. The Boss then hit its 6-request limit, so Customer Service got "boss could not answer" and marked itself not confident. That CS → Boss chain took about 91 seconds of a roughly 140-second run, and the whole ticket used about twice the input tokens of 101 or 102.

What I liked is that nothing broke. Every safeguard fired the way it was meant to, Customer Service admitted it wasn't confident instead of making something up, and the Boss's own final call still worked: don't quote a discount yet, ask the club what percentage they want, offer the 8 mediums at $58, and don't promise the other 12. The downsides were lots of repeated work (5 get_price_and_margin calls and 3 check_stock calls), and Inventory never called get_vendor_lead_time and said the lead time was unknown. That's partly fair, since the vendors table doesn't say which vendor makes hoodies. Also, "resolved" here only means the team made a decision and nothing needed paying. The club still owes us an answer, and our ticket statuses can't show "waiting on customer."

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools

Honestly, 101 and 102 would have been simpler, cheaper and easier to debug as one agent with the same MCP tools. Ticket 101 is basically four lookups (stock, invoice, vendor lead time, cash) and one payment. The five-agent version spent 6 steps and 23,318 input tokens on it, and the specialist that should have added the most, Accounting, actually checked less than a single careful agent would have. Ticket 102 is one lease lookup and one balance check. Instead, Facilities and Accounting asked each other the same question and needed the loop guard to stop a circular ask. The extra complexity there came from the multi-agent design itself, not from the ticket.

Specialization still helped in a few places. Customer Service can't see cash or margins, so its reply to Tauhid couldn't leak anything it shouldn't, and its replies were the most "send-ready" thing the team produced. On 103, having separate views of stock, margin and the customer reply was useful, and Customer Service marking itself not confident is a nice built-in honesty check. But the most important safety property, that no agent can pay anything, comes from the tools and the approve route, not from having five agents. My take: for a shop this size, one main agent with all the tools, a separate customer-writing agent, and the same human approval step would get most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost. Five agents make more sense once roles truly need different permissions or someone separate has to review the work.

5. Three problems the team can't solve yet, and what we'd add

Actually completing Tauhid's sale

Once the S reprint arrives, Tauhid still needs to buy the tee. The team can't do that. There's no orders or sales table, nothing can reserve or take stock off the shelf, and cash can only go down through payments, so a sale can't be recorded as money coming in. We'd need tools like create_order, reserve_stock, record_sale (stock down, cash up) and mark_shipped, plus an orders table. Since this touches stock, incoming cash and the customer, I'd add a new Orders specialist rather than stretching Customer Service, and keep refunds behind human approval.

Ordering and receiving a restock

Ticket 103 is 12 hoodies short, and the team can't order them. The vendors table has no link to products and no prices, so Inventory couldn't even tell which vendor makes hoodies. Invoice 501 was $840 for a rush reprint of an unknown quantity, so unit cost × quantity isn't a real vendor quote. There's also no way to log a purchase order or add stock when a delivery shows up. We'd need get_vendor_quote (backed by a vendor price list that links products to vendors), create_purchase_order (human-approved like payments) and receive_shipment(sku, size, qty) to update inventory. Inventory could own this, with Accounting checking cash first, so no new agent is needed at first.

Following up with customers

On 103 the next step is literally "ask Yale AI Club what discount they want," and the team can't do it. Customer Service can only draft text: it can't send a message, read a reply, or mark the ticket as waiting on the customer. Ticket status is only open or resolved, so 103 shows resolved even though the deal isn't finished, and new requests can't come in without someone adding a ticket row. We'd need send_customer_message (with an outbox a person reviews), list_customer_replies, update_ticket (including a "waiting on customer" status and notes) and create_ticket for intake. Customer Service already owns the wording, so it can own sending, with the Boss controlling status changes. No new agent is needed.
